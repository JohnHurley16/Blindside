# What happened here

The backstory, decided. What the works were, why the water came in, why nobody switched the
machinery off, what it has been surveying for a hundred years, and how a player finds all of
that out without reading a word of it.

Written 2026-09-09 against the designer's note: *"people should really want to play to figure
out what is going on, hint at the backstory, build up all the key points."*

**This is not a story.** `DESIGN.html` is explicit — *"There is no story and no ending.
Deadwater has the structure of a fishing trip, not a campaign"* — and nothing below changes
that. There is no sequence, no gating, no act structure, and a player who never looks at a
single cast mark plays the entire game and loses nothing. What this document designs is a
**state**: the world is one arithmetic, frozen, still running, and every part of the answer is
already in the frame for anyone who looks. The mystery is a second axis made entirely of
objects the art direction was going to build anyway.

**Status of everything here.** `THE-MACHINERY.md` §1–§8 and `ART-DIRECTION.md` §1, §5, §6, §7
are treated as fixed and are never contradicted; where I extend a section I name it. Everything
that is mine is a proposal, marked, and collected in §9. The yes/no questions are in §10.

---

## 0. The three rules this document obeys

1. **Nothing contradicts `THE-MACHINERY.md` or `ART-DIRECTION.md`.** Extensions name their
   section. Two places where those two documents (or the vision boards) already disagree with
   each other are called out in §8 rather than papered over.
2. **No text the player reads as prose.** No journals, no logs, no diaries, no signage in
   language. The world's entire written output is **numbers stamped into iron**, and there is
   not one sentence anywhere in it. This is a hard constraint and it does more work than any
   amount of writing would: it is why the place reads as *found* rather than *authored*, and it
   is the reason the deepest secret in the game is a shape rather than a word (§2.4).
3. **Every beat cashes out as an object, a place, or an event** a generator can place and an
   artist can build. §6 is the audit of that, and any beat above that has no row in §6 should be
   struck.

## 1. What actually happened

### 1.1 The whole of it, plainly

An iron field. A company worked it downward for a long time — long enough that the shallow
workings are natural karst opened out with a bit of rail in them and the deep ones are machine
ground. Iron is cheap, so the operation only ever worked because it was efficient, and its
single largest cost was **pumping**. You can only work below the water table where you can keep
the water off (`THE-MACHINERY.md` §7). Every metre down doubled down on that bet.

Ahead of the drives, the company ran a **survey**. Machines bolted into the bedrock, water-driven,
each one hammering the rock on a fixed cycle and listening to what came back, so that the drives
could be driven at ore instead of at rock. That is why the tunnels go where they go and why the
deposits are where they are — canon, `THE-MACHINERY.md` §7. The survey is the reason this mine
was ever profitable, and the survey is the oldest continuously operating thing on the site.

Then the arithmetic turned over. The ore that could be reached stopped paying for the water it
cost to reach it. Not in an afternoon — over years, as the workings got deeper and wetter and
the price of iron did what the price of iron does.

**So they wound down.** This is the part that matters, and it is deliberately undramatic:

- Nobody fled. Nothing exploded. No shaft collapsed with a shift in it.
- The last crews took out what was worth taking and left what was not. That is why there is a
  footing in the deep ground with an anvil, a bolt ring, a drip joint and a rising main, and **no
  mast** (§6.4) — a machine was unbolted and carried up the shaft, because a machine is worth
  money and a foundation is not.
- The pumps were the last thing to stop, and they stopped because there was no more money to run
  them, not because anything broke. The water came up slowly, over months and then years, and
  stopped where it stopped. That is the tide mark: a level that moved and then didn't.
- **Nobody died here.** There are no remains, no bodies, no personal effects, ever, anywhere in
  this game. The only corpses in this world are machines and they are yours. This forecloses the
  horror reading, and it is what keeps the place *a works and not a temple* — `THE-MACHINERY.md`
  §7's own rule, which I am here to keep rather than to bend.

> **Nothing dramatic happened here. That is the most unsettling thing about it.**

And then, over a century, the water made the world the art direction already specifies: cast iron
graphitised black below the line and intact in silhouette, a mineral crust above it, flowstone
over the shallow marks, everything ruined except the four or five surfaces something is still
rubbing.

### 1.2 Why the machinery was left running

**Because a running survey is how you hold ground, and holding the ground was the last asset the
company had.**

Ground is held by working it. Stop working it and it lapses to whoever comes next. A survey is
work. So the last thing this company did with the money it had left was **convert its mine into
a machine that would keep its claim alive without it**: a ring of survey machines that need
nobody, cannot be switched off by anybody who is not standing there, and are powered by the exact
process that ruined the mine.

Every detail of the Assayer that is already canon is downstream of that intent:

| the established fact | what it means under this answer |
|---|---|
| water-driven, header tank filled by the flood (`THE-MACHINERY.md` §1, §7) | *deliberate*, not lucky. You do not accidentally build a survey machine that runs on the thing that is drowning you. You build one that way when it has to run after you have gone |
| bolted into bedrock, no fuel, no store (§1) | it was never meant to be moved, refuelled or attended |
| the fixed 36° index step, always the same direction (§5) | it is *covering the claim*, not investigating anything. A survey that must be seen to be continuous walks the whole compass and never stops |
| "still trying to file a survey report to an office that is not there" (§6) | filing **is** the work. The report is the proof, and proof is the point |
| "it never needed anybody" (§1) | the specification, not an accident of construction |

**Why "they forgot" is not merely a weak answer but a factually wrong one, and the player can
prove it.** The Assayers are **the newest things in the mine.** They carry the highest item
numbers in every block (§3), their castings are the sharpest, and they stand in the ground that
paid worst. Nobody forgets to switch off the most expensive thing they ever built. Something you
install *last*, in ground that does not pay, that runs without you, that cannot be turned off —
is not an oversight. It is an instruction.

**The object that carries this, and it is the best one in the document: there is no off switch,
and there never was.** *(Proposal; extends `ART-DIRECTION.md` §5.2 and the machinery board's
proposal 3.)*

Look at an Assayer for five minutes and there is nothing on it a person could operate. No lever,
no valve within reach, no cock on the rising main, no release on the brake band, no isolator, no
handle. The ladder up the mast (machinery board proposal 3) goes to the winch and ends at a
bearing with grease points and nothing else — it was for the people who *fitted* it, and they
left nothing behind to work.

This only reads if the player has something to read it against, so it is a **placement rule**:
**every other station family object has a control and the Assayers do not.** The hoist signal box
has a hinged cover and a lever. The pump has a throttle wheel. The rail switch is a lever and a
counterweight. The Bus junction box has a latch. A player who has downloaded from three of those
and then stands at an Assayer has met the answer, by contrast, without being told. *You cannot
turn it off. That was the point.*

It also settles a question the design will be asked eventually — *can I disable the hazard?* No,
and the reason is visible on the casting.

### 1.3 What this deliberately does not say

- **No name.** Not for the company, not for the field, not for the place. The world has no
  language in it, so a name could only arrive as prose, and prose is forbidden. The player can
  never learn who these people were, and that is correct: `DESIGN.html`'s *"specificity ages
  badly"* is a design rule with an in-world reason.
- **No date, no era, no calendar.** `ART-DIRECTION.md` §5.2 already forbids dates on castings.
  §3 makes that rule load-bearing rather than arbitrary: the index numbers are the only
  chronology this world has, so the world tells time by **counting**, which is why the index is
  worth collecting at all.
- **No substance.** Nothing here names what is in the ore or what the survey found. §2.4 gives
  that refusal a physical cause.
- **No lost civilisation.** `THE-MACHINERY.md` §7: *"A works, not a temple."* Everything above
  is a business failing.

## 2. What the survey is for

The deepest layer. Most players will never reach it and that is the design.

### 2.1 The client

The survey files to a survey office. The office existed exactly as long as the company did.
`THE-MACHINERY.md` §6 already states the consequence: *"It is still trying to file a survey
report to an office that is not there, and it answers anything that knocks back, because as far
as it is concerned you are the office."*

So the client, in the sense that matters, is **whoever eventually buys the claim.** The survey's
purpose was to *value the ground for a sale* — to say how much was down there, so that the thing
the company could not work could at least be sold. Nobody came.

That makes the download the transaction it has always mechanically been. `yields()` returns
`None` after one take, forever (`THE-MACHINERY.md` §6). Under this answer that is not a
gameplay limiter dressed up: **it is the report being filed.** A spent Assayer — boom parked on
its last bearing, hammer down, tank drained, no glow ever again (`ART-DIRECTION.md` §6.4) —
stops meaning *someone beat me here* and starts meaning *that one is finished. It waited a
hundred years and somebody finally came.*

### 2.2 What it was actually listening for

An Assayer is a seismic weight drop: 1.6 t of cast iron falling onto bedrock, ~56–81 kJ into the
rock, and a directional array listening to the return (`ART-DIRECTION.md` §5.1). That instrument
cannot assay anything. **It measures structure.** Depth to an interface, the dip of a horizon,
where a reflector stops.

So the survey was never looking for ore. It was looking for **the thing that put the ore there**,
and for how far it goes. The mine's deposits sit on a structure; the survey has been walking the
compass at 36° a cycle, block by block, mapping that structure's extent — how deep it lies, on
what bearings it continues, and where it ends.

### 2.3 Did it find it

**Yes, and the answer is that it does not end.**

Every bearing for a century has returned the same thing: the reflector is still there, it is
still dipping, and it is deeper than the last one. The structure the mine worked the top of runs
below every working, below the water table, past the edge of the claim, and under the ground the
pit-head is standing on.

The company knew this before the pumps stopped. **They failed on the shallowest, poorest,
wettest edge of something they had already measured.** That is why they held the ground: not
sentiment, arithmetic. What they were sitting on was worth more than the mine that failed on top
of it, and the only thing between them and it was water they could not afford to lift.

### 2.4 What it would mean, and why the game can never name it

Three things, in ascending order of how much they recontextualise:

1. **The loot gradient is not a game rule; it is the survey's own finding.** `DESIGN-PRINCIPLES.md`
   §2 says the cool things are found by depth. `ART-DIRECTION.md` §7 says the mine *worked
   downward and got better as it went.* This answer is the reason both are true, and it means the
   deep chambers are richer **because the survey said to drive there**, not because a designer
   weighted a table.
2. **The extraction loop is the same failure, smaller and faster.** You go down, take a little,
   and come back before the water and the clock beat you. That is what the company did, at a
   scale of decades, and it is what killed them. Your machines are better and your margin is
   thinner. `THE-MACHINERY.md` §7 already wrote the line this earns: *"Your agents are the same
   idea, worse."*
3. **The instrument that found it cannot say what it is.** A weight drop measures a reflector.
   It gets depth, dip and continuity, and it gets nothing about composition — a seismic survey
   has never once told anybody what a rock is made of. So the deepest secret in this game is a
   **shape and a size and a depth**, and the machine that has been guarding it for a hundred
   years does not know what it found either.

That is the physical cause for `DESIGN.html`'s refusal to name the substance. The game will never
tell you what is down there, and the reason is not coyness: **nobody ever knew.** *(Proposal, and
the one I am most pleased with; it converts a stated non-decision into a fact about an
instrument.)*

### 2.5 The inversion at the bottom of it

The Assayer runs on the flood. The flood is why nobody could reach what the Assayer found. **The
answer is powered by the reason the answer is useless**, and it will go on being powered for as
long as it rains.

That is also why the trailer's first and last images are both water (§7).

## 3. The index

`K 14` raised on an anvil, `K 14 · 2` on a hammer, cast index plates on the wall of every worked
passage at 1.40 m and 4.8 m spacing (`ART-DIRECTION.md` §6.5, `THE-MACHINERY.md`, the machinery
and cave vision boards). This is the best diegetic collectible the world has and nothing
currently uses it. Here is the system.

### 3.1 The grammar

```
<letter>  <item>  · <part or station>          e.g.  K 14      K 14 · 2      K 7 · 41
```

| element | what it denotes |
|---|---|
| **letter** | a **block of the claim** — a parcel of ground the company held. Big: hundreds of metres across, many chambers, a whole match's cave |
| **number** | the **installation order within that block**. Item 1 is the first thing the company put in block K; item 40 is the fortieth |
| **· n** | a **part of that item** where the item is an assembly, or a **station along it** where the item is a linear work (a drive, a road, a bus) |
| **the foundry ring** | a round mark with *n* bars — the **series**. The claim outgrew the alphabet; the bars are how you know which K you are looking at |

Worked examples, all consistent with what is already drawn:

- **`K 14`** on the anvil. The anvil is the item's datum — it is the piece bolted to bedrock, so
  it carries the bare number. Everything else on the machine is a part of it.
- **`K 14 · 2`** on the hammer. The second part fitted after the anvil went down. Canon, and now
  it means something.
- **`K 7 · 41`** on a wall plate: block K, item 7 (a *drive* is an installed item too), station 41
  along it. At 4.8 m spacing that is the forty-first plate since the drive's mouth.

**Two consequences a player can act on:**

1. **The number is the age, and therefore the depth.** The mine worked downward and got better as
   it went, so low numbers are shallow, crude, corroded and half-buried in flowstone; high numbers
   are deep, sharp, competent and wet. A player who has read six plates can date a place before
   they see what is in it. This is `ART-DIRECTION.md` §7's cue 5 — *the marks get younger* — made
   **numerical** as well as material. It is a seventh consumer of the one BFS depth field, not a
   new system.
2. **The letter is where, and only coarsely.** Which parcel of ground, not which room.

### 3.2 Why it is not a wayfinding grid, and cannot become a third Fix source

`ART-DIRECTION.md` §6.5 is a hard rule: *"No permanent honest wayfinding grid. Nothing in the
world may act as a third Fix source and compete with the beacon."* The index must survive that
rule rather than negotiate with it. Three independent reasons it does:

1. **The letters are not laid out on the ground.** Blocks were lettered in the order the company
   *acquired* them, not in the order they lie. K is not next to L. The letter has no metric
   content of any kind; it is a filing key. *(Proposal, and it is the load-bearing one.)*
2. **The plate numbers are ordinal along a line whose start you do not know.** `K 7 · 41` tells
   you nothing until you have already mapped drive K 7, at which point your own odometry told you
   the same thing, better.
3. **The index never reaches `Belief` at all.** `Return::Optical` carries occupancy, not radiance
   and not glyphs (`ART-DIRECTION.md` §8). Raised numerals on a 160 × 100 mm casting are not
   resolvable by a scanning range sensor. **A policy can never read an index mark.** It is a
   *player-side* and *replay-side* signal only, which is exactly the layer where a collectible
   belongs and exactly the layer that cannot corrupt a pose estimate.

### 3.3 How a player actually gets it

Two channels, and they are different things:

- **The marks are the clue.** They are everywhere, they are free, they explain nothing, and they
  accumulate into a nagging pattern. A player sees maybe forty of them before they care.
- **The register is the key**, and it comes out of machines by **download** (§4). Every machine
  in the mine had to file, they all file into the ground and along the Bus, and they have all been
  listening to each other for a century — so each one holds a partial copy of the register
  assembled from its neighbours. **Deeper machines are newer, so they heard more, so they hold
  more.** The register is reconstructed by triangulation across many downloads, and no single
  source ever gives you a whole block. *(Proposal.)*

Nothing is ever prised off a wall. `ART-DIRECTION.md` §6.4 rules that a discovery is a
transaction and not a pickup, and §9 forbids a glowing item on the floor; both hold here.

### 3.4 Where it lives on the surface, and what it looks like

**The tally board in the winding house.** *(Proposal.)* A real mine keeps a board of numbered
hooks in the lamp room and knows who is underground by which hooks are empty. This is that
object: the prior industry's own cast board, found on the wall of the roofless winding house, a
grid of slots with block letters down one edge and item numbers across, almost all of them empty.

The player fills it. Each recovered register row drops a cast tab into a slot. It is the
collectible's entire UI and it is a wall in a shed rather than a menu — which is also the only
form `DESIGN.html` permits, since *"Progression is what you learned and what you built, not a
level number."* A tally board is literally what you learned, hung up.

It sits beside the bench where recovered wrecks are read (surface board, `12_yard_wreck.png`) and
is the same gesture: **things come up the shaft and get read on the surface.**

### 3.5 What it unlocks

| you hold | you get |
|---|---|
| **any two rows in one block** | the shape of the system: you can read *older/newer* and *shallower/deeper* off a mark before you reach it |
| **a block's page** (most of its item list) | **pre-raid intel**: entering a cave in that block, the launch map names which station families are down there and roughly how deep. Intel is the currency of an extraction game and this is the diegetic way to sell it |
| **the count for a block** | how much of it you have not found. An incomplete page reads as incomplete without promising specific unshipped content |
| **enough blocks to see the pattern** | the layer-4 realisation (§5): *the last item installed in every single block is an Assayer, and they sit on a line.* Nothing states this. It falls out of rows |
| **the summary sheet** (§4, station 6) | the answer to §2. It is unreadable — literally uninterpretable as data — until you hold enough index to know what block and bearing it refers to. That is the gate between layer 4 and layer 5, and it is earned by play, not by reading |

**And one thing it should not unlock yet.** The register could name blocks the generator does not
generate — the season hook is obvious and the temptation is strong. It promises content that does
not exist, so the default is **no** until Phase 9, where `ROADMAP.md` already plans *"shift
generation weights, change an ancient system's state, publish the transition as an event."* At
that point a new block letter appearing on a hundred players' tally boards **is** the event, and
it costs nothing to ship. Question 8 in §10.

## 4. What is in a download

### 4.1 The rule that generates every block, forever

`THE-MACHINERY.md` §6: a download is *"the plant's own control software."* `ART-DIRECTION.md`
§6.4: *"each new block ships with an ancient system that yields it."* Neither says *why* a
century-old mining machine's software would hand a modern robot a predicate. Here is the why, and
it is the content template:

> **Every machine in this mine was an autonomous machine that had to answer one question in the
> dark without a person. The block you get from it is that question.**

A pump had to know whether it was losing. A signal box had to know whether the road was occupied.
A switch had to know which road it had been told to use. An Assayer had to know when it was about
to fire. To design a new base block, design the machine that needed it — and you have designed
its station, its silhouette, its depth band and its download in one move.

This satisfies `DESIGN-PRINCIPLES.md` §1 on both counts: blocks arrive as data, and the player
**meets each one in play before composing it** — you survive the shock before you are handed
`shock_imminent`, and you watch a pump lose before you are handed *losing ground*.

*(Note for the engineers: naming specific blocks here is content, not code. `DESIGN-PRINCIPLES.md`
§1 forbids anything downstream — induction, tree render, VM, editor — being written over a fixed
set by name. Nothing below asks for that. This is a list, and adding to it is a data change.)*

### 4.2 The ladder, by depth

### 4.3 What a download is never

- Never a pickup, never an object on the floor, never a glow (`ART-DIRECTION.md` §6.4, §9).
- Never a weapon, never a stat, never a chassis part. It is a **question a machine had to answer**,
  which is to say a predicate, an action, or a page.
- Never free of the risk. Coupling is the download rate and coupling is the dose
  (`THE-MACHINERY.md` §6). The reward channel is a broadcast at `HEAR_ANCIENT_RANGE` and the whole
  cave hears you take it.

## 5. The layers of the mystery

Ordered by what a player learns in their first hour, their tenth, their hundredth. Each layer is
reached by **playing**, and each **recontextualises** the one before it rather than adding to it.

### Layer 1 — hour 1: *"there is a machine down there and it is still working."*

**Learned by:** hearing a ratchet in the dark before seeing anything; walking into the scour and
reading *nothing settles here* forty seconds before finding out why; watching the boom swing and
stop; getting hit; learning that rock is not cover and the flank is; downloading `shock_imminent`
from the thing that nearly killed you.

**What it establishes:** this is not a ruin. It is a workplace that never closed. Everything is
dead except four surfaces something is still rubbing, and those are polished bright.

**Recontextualises:** nothing. This is the floor, and most players stop here happily.

### Layer 2 — hours 2–10: *"they are not individuals. They are numbered inventory."*

**Learned by:** finding a second machine and seeing the same shape of mark on it; reading `K 9` on
a pump plinth and `K 7 · 41` on a wall and realising the wall plates and the anvil speak the same
grammar; noticing that the numbers go *up* as the passages get straighter and the marks get
sharper; and — the good one — hitting a **contradiction**: two `K 14`s with a different number of
bars on the foundry ring.

**Recontextualises layer 1:** the machine you survived is not *the machinery*. It is **item 14**.
There are thirteen more in that block, at least, and there are other blocks. A hazard becomes an
inventory, and the size of the place multiplies under you without a single new room being built.

### Layer 3 — hours 10–40: *"they were left running on purpose."*

**Learned by:** counting. The register rows say the Assayers carry the **highest item numbers in
every block** — installed last. `ART-DIRECTION.md` §7's own gradient says the deep ground got
better and the deep ore got thinner. Which means the company was still installing its most
expensive machines, in its best workmanship, in ground that was no longer paying. Then you stand
at one and notice that **there is nothing on it a person could operate**, and you have just come
from a pump with a throttle wheel and a switch with a lever.

**Recontextualises layer 2:** the index is not a maintenance system. It is the record of a company
that kept building instruments after it stopped mining. The question stops being *what is this
place* and becomes *what were they doing here at the end.*

### Layer 4 — hours 40–100: *"they were holding the ground."*

**Learned by:** holding enough of the register to see that the last item in *every* block is the
same kind of machine; by finding a **boundary chamber** — an Assayer standing in a driven chamber
with no working face, no muck pile, no rails, nothing to mine, and a waist-high cast post beside
it carrying a letter and a bar and nothing else; and by recognising that post, because there is an
identical one at the edge of your own yard that has been in every frame for a hundred hours.

**Recontextualises everything:** the hazard is a **legal instrument**. It has been doing paperwork
for a century and the paperwork is done with a 1.6-tonne hammer. And the download stops being
looting: when you knock, it files, and it stops forever. **You are the office.** A spent Assayer
is not *someone beat me here* — it is *that one is finished.*

### Layer 5 — hours 100+: *"and it found something."*

**Learned by:** reaching a machine below the water table, in clear water over a drowned scour,
with the highest number anyone has ever read, and holding enough index that its summary sheet
resolves into something rather than noise.

**Recontextualises layer 4:** the claim they were holding was not a failed iron mine. They had
already measured what was under it and it does not end. They died holding a ticket they could not
cash because the water cost more than the ore, and they left a machine to hold it until somebody
could lift the water. **You are the somebody**, and your whole loop — go down, take a little, get
out before the clock and the water beat you — is their failure at 1/100 scale, run by better
machines, failing the same way for the same reason.

`THE-MACHINERY.md` §7 already wrote the last line of this: *"Your agents are the same idea,
worse."* You teach a machine, send it somewhere you cannot reach, and it works alone on your
behalf until something stops it. The Assayer is what that looks like after a hundred years.

### The audit

| layer | reached by | recontextualises by | is it playable, not readable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | surviving the cycle | — | yes: a hazard with a mechanism |
| 2 | seeing a second mark | scale | yes: two objects, no text |
| 3 | counting item numbers; the missing control | intent | yes: numbers and an absence |
| 4 | the register's pattern; the boundary post | purpose | yes: a place and an object you already knew |
| 5 | the deepest station; the summary | the whole world | yes: a chamber you have to be equipped to enter |

## 9. Everything I guessed

Per `CLAUDE.md`. Every item below is mine and none of it is in any existing doc.

1. That ground is held by working it, that a survey is work, and that this is why the machinery
   was left running (§1.2). **The load-bearing guess of the entire document.**
2. That the mine wound down on a margin rather than failing in an event; that the pumps stopped
   for money; that the water rose over years (§1.1).
3. That nobody died here and there are no human remains anywhere in the game (§1.1).

   G5).
5. That the survey measures structure and cannot measure composition, and that this is the
   in-world reason the substance is never named (§2.2, §2.4).
6. That the survey's answer is *it does not end*, and that the company knew before they stopped
   (§2.3).
7. The index grammar: letter = block of claim, number = installation order within block, `· n` =
   part or station, foundry bars = series (§3.1).
8. That blocks are lettered by acquisition order and are therefore not spatially ordered — which
   is what keeps the index clear of `ART-DIRECTION.md` §6.5 (§3.2).
9. That machines hold partial copies of the register from listening to each other for a century,
   and that deeper machines hold more (§3.3).
10. The tally board in the winding house as the collectible's surface UI (§3.4).
11. That holding a block's page buys pre-raid intel (§3.5).
12. The whole download ladder: six stations, their depth bands, and the five blocks named
    (`contact_heard`, `losing_ground`, `shock_imminent`, `follow_workings`, `hurt`). Three of
    those five are already on `CAVE-BLOCKS.md` §6's deferred list; **`losing_ground` and
    `follow_workings` are new blocks I have invented** and they need the same scrutiny any block
    gets (§4.2).
13. The generative rule that a block is the question its machine had to answer alone (§4.1).
14. The posting board, the boundary monument, the empty footing, the boundary chamber, the
    underwater scour, and the winding-house engine bed carrying a low item number (§6).
15. That the drip lands on the index mark and is why it is legible (§6.3).
16. Every depth band and every rarity number in §6.7. None of them is measured; they are chosen so
    that layers 4 and 5 take tens of hours rather than one raid, and they are pure tuning.
17. The five trailer beats in §7, and specifically that the download shot should fill with **rows
